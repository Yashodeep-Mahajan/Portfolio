The Kepler Planets: Our Cosmic Blind Date with Alien Worlds

By Yashodeep Mahajan | April 09, 2020

Imagine signing up for a cosmic dating app where each profile is a new planet, complete with stats like size, temperature, and “likelihood of hosting life.” Swipe right for oceans, swipe left for molten lava. Thanks to NASA’s Kepler Space Telescope, we’ve been speed-dating the galaxy for over a decade, uncovering thousands of exoplanets, planets outside our solar system that range from “maybe habitable” to “definitely deadly.” If the universe is a party, the Kepler planets are the guests who might just make things interesting.

What Is the Kepler Space Telescope, Anyway?
Launched in 2009, the Kepler Space Telescope was basically an interstellar paparazzi, snapping photos of distant stars to catch them dimming ever so slightly. This dimming happens when a planet crosses in front of its star, blocking a tiny bit of light, a move known as the transit method. By measuring these dips in brightness, Kepler could figure out a planet’s size, orbit, and even whether it was in the Goldilocks Zone, not too hot, not too cold, just right for liquid water. Kepler was a relentless matchmaker, scanning over 150,000 stars and confirming more than 2,600 planets before retiring in 2018. In human terms, it was like sifting through every dating profile on Earth, figuring out who might actually be worth getting coffee with. And just like in dating, most of what Kepler found was a bit…meh. But then there were the gems, the ones that made us think, “Wait, could this actually work?”

Hot Jupiters and Other Weirdos
If you thought our solar system was the standard, surprise! The Kepler planets blew that idea to smithereens. For starters, there are the Hot Jupiters, gas giants that orbit so close to their stars they practically get a cosmic sunburn. These planets have years that last mere days and temperatures hot enough to melt metal. Imagine a Jupiter-like planet zooming around its star faster than you can get through your Monday emails. Then there are the super-Earths, which sound like they should come with capes. These are rocky planets bigger than Earth but smaller than Neptune, and some might even have atmospheres. If we ever need a backup planet, one of these might just make the cut, assuming they’re not covered in volcanoes or acid rain. And don’t get me started on rogue planets, worlds that have been kicked out of their star systems and wander the galaxy alone. They’re basically the stray cats of space, and while they’re not part of the Kepler haul, they remind us just how weird things can get out there.

The Ones That Got Away
Not every planet in the Kepler series is a winner. Some are orbiting stars that flare up unpredictably, blasting their surfaces with radiation. Others have orbits so elongated that they spend part of their year as frozen wastelands and the rest baking like rotisserie chickens. There are even planets that orbit two stars like Tatooine in Star Wars which sounds cool until you realize the weather forecast would be twice as confusing.

Kepler-186f: Earth’s Distant Cousin
In 2014, Kepler hit the jackpot with Kepler-186f, the first Earth-sized planet found in the habitable zone of its star. Cue the sci-fi theme music. Located about 500 light-years away in the constellation Cygnus, Kepler-186f is like Earth’s long-lost cousin, the one who lives far away but shows up on Facebook every once in a while. While it’s too far to visit anytime soon (or ever, unless warp drive becomes a thing), Kepler-186f is tantalizing because it might have liquid water. Its red dwarf star is cooler and dimmer than our Sun, which means any plants there would likely be red or even black to soak up as much light as possible. Imagine a forest of dark, alien Christmas trees festive, but also a little goth.

Kepler-452b: Super-Earth or Super-Hot Mess?
If Kepler-186f is Earth’s cousin, then Kepler-452b is that older sibling who seems to have their life together. Discovered in 2015, Kepler-452b is about 60% larger than Earth and orbits in the habitable zone of a Sun-like star. Scientists dubbed it “Earth 2.0,” which might be a bit of a stretch especially since it’s a lot heavier and might have a thick atmosphere that could make things unbearably steamy. Think Earth, but with a permanent sauna setting. Still, the idea of a bigger, possibly greener Earth has its appeal. If we ever get tired of this one (or, you know, ruin it), Kepler-452b might be worth the interstellar move if you don’t mind a few thousand years of travel time.

Kepler taught us that planets are everywhere, pretty much every star has at least one. That’s a big deal because it means the odds of finding life out there might be a lot better than we thought. It also made us rethink our definition of a “habitable” planet. Maybe life doesn’t need a twin of Earth to thrive maybe it’s out there in places we wouldn’t expect, sipping methane cocktails and laughing at our narrow expectations. Since Kepler’s retirement, the search has continued with TESS (Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite) and other missions, but its legacy lives on. Kepler turned the galaxy from a mostly empty canvas into a vibrant mosaic of worlds, each with its own quirks and possibilities. So the next time you look up at the night sky, remember that somewhere out there might be a planet with oceans, forests, or something even stranger waiting for us to swipe right. The universe is a big place, and thanks to Kepler, we know it’s a lot less lonely than we thought.
