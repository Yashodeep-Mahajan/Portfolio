What Is Dark Matter?

By Yashodeep Mahajan | May 15, 2020

Imagine walking into a room where everything is invisible, the furniture, the walls, even the floor but you can still feel them. That’s how astronomers feel about dark matter. We can’t see it, touch it, or directly detect it, but we know it’s there because it affects the universe in ways we can measure.

Dark matter is a mysterious, invisible substance that makes up about 85% of all matter in the universe. Unlike normal matter (like the stuff that makes up you, me, planets, and stars), dark matter does not emit, absorb, or reflect light. This makes it impossible to observe with traditional telescopes. However, its gravitational pull is so strong that it influences the movement of galaxies, bends light, and shapes the universe as we know it. If dark matter were to suddenly disappear, galaxies would fly apart, stars wouldn’t form the way they do, and the entire structure of the universe would be completely different. But if we can’t see it, how did we even discover its existence?

How Did We Discover Dark Matter?
The idea of dark matter was first proposed in the 1930s by Swiss astronomer Fritz Zwicky. While observing the Coma Cluster of galaxies, Zwicky noticed something puzzling. The galaxies were moving so quickly that they should have flown apart if the only matter present was what we could see. He calculated that there had to be far more mass holding the cluster together than what was visible. He called this missing mass “dunkle Materie,” which is German for dark matter. In the 1970s, further evidence for dark matter came from the work of American astronomer Vera Rubin. Rubin studied the rotation curves of spiral galaxies and found that stars on the outskirts of galaxies were moving just as fast as those near the center. According to the known laws of physics, stars farther from the center should move more slowly if only visible matter were present. The fact that they didn’t suggested that an unseen mass was providing the necessary gravitational pull.

What Is Dark Matter Made Of?
Despite the compelling evidence for dark matter, we still don’t know what it’s made of. Scientists have proposed several candidates, the most popular being Weakly Interacting Massive Particles (WIMPs). These hypothetical particles interact through gravity but not through electromagnetic forces, making them invisible to our instruments. Other candidates include axions and sterile neutrinos, but none have been detected directly. Numerous experiments are underway to uncover the true nature of dark matter. Particle accelerators like the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) attempt to create dark matter particles by smashing protons together at high speeds. Meanwhile, underground detectors are designed to catch glimpses of dark matter particles passing through the Earth. So far, these efforts have yet to yield definitive results, but the search continues.

Why Does Dark Matter Matter?
Understanding dark matter is crucial because it holds the key to unraveling many of the universe’s mysteries. It influences galaxy formation, cosmic expansion, and even the fate of the universe itself. Without dark matter, our current models of the universe would fall apart. Moreover, dark matter’s gravitational effects act as a kind of cosmic scaffolding, providing the structure needed for galaxies and clusters to form and stay together. If we can determine what dark matter is made of, it could open new doors in physics and potentially redefine our understanding of matter and energy.

Dark matter serves as a reminder of how much we still have to learn about the cosmos. As we continue to build more powerful telescopes and detectors, we inch closer to uncovering the secrets of this invisible substance that holds the universe together.
