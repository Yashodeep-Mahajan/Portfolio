The Philosophy of Time: It’s About Time We Talked About It!

By Yashodeep Mahajan | June 27, 2020

Time. It’s that thing we’re constantly running out of. It’s what makes us late for meetings, makes our coffee cold, and makes us stare at the clock during boring lectures. We’re obsessed with it, we measure it, and we spend half our lives thinking about how we wish we could spend it differently. But here’s the thing, philosophers have been having some wild and deep thoughts about time long before we were stressing about deadlines. So let’s take a quick (okay, maybe not that quick) dive into the philosophy of time, a world where time doesn’t just fly, it bends, stretches, and occasionally messes with your head.

What Even Is Time?
Before we get into the thick stuff, let’s tackle the most basic question: What is time, anyway? It seems simple, right? Time is what keeps everything from happening at once. It’s that “tick-tock” on the clock, the hours that slip by when you’re too engrossed in a Netflix binge, the calendar flipping pages faster than you can keep up with. But ask a philosopher, and you’re in for a wild ride of interpretations. Time could be a straight line, a loop, a social construct, or even an illusion. It’s basically the ultimate cosmic riddle. In one corner, we have Newtonian time, that’s the kind of time we all know and love. It’s linear, constant, and unwavering. You know, the kind where 1 second is always 1 second, no matter where you are in the universe. But wait! Not so fast. In the other corner, we’ve got Einstein’s time, which isn’t as predictable. Einstein said that time is more like a flexible rubber band that can stretch and bend depending on where you are and how fast you’re moving. So, time isn’t so absolute after all. In fact, it can move at different speeds depending on gravity and velocity. Try explaining that to your alarm clock.

Past, Present, and Future: Are They All Real?
Here’s the big one, the nature of time. Is time like a river, flowing in one direction, carrying us along with it? Or is time more like a painting, where all moments exist simultaneously, and we just happen to experience them in a linear way? This debate has been at the heart of metaphysical philosophy of time for centuries. Let’s break it down into two schools of thought, Presentism and Eternalism. Presentism says that only the present moment is real. The past is gone, and the future hasn’t arrived yet, so all we really have is the “now.” So, when you’re procrastinating on something important, the past doesn’t exist, and neither does the future, only your very real, very present need to check your social media feed. Eternalism, on the other hand, takes a much broader view. It posits that all times, past, present, and future are equally real. This means that the moment you’re experiencing now is just as real as the one from 10 years ago or the one that hasn’t happened yet. Kind of makes you feel like your life is one giant movie, and you’re just watching the scenes play out in order. If that doesn’t blow your mind, consider this: If time is eternal, does that mean the past is still around, just as real as when it happened? Can you visit it? Can you change it? Are we just stuck in the middle of the movie, hoping for a good plot twist?

Time Travel: Philosophical Dream or Cosmic Nightmare?
Let’s talk about time travel, everyone’s favorite fantasy and worst nightmare. Is it possible to travel back in time and alter the course of history? What would happen if you could go forward and see your future self, awkwardly giving you unsolicited life advice? Well, philosophers have definitely had something to say about this. One of the classic time-travel paradoxes is the Grandfather Paradox. The idea is simple, you travel back in time and accidentally stop your grandfather from meeting your grandmother. If that happens, you’re never born, which means you couldn’t have traveled back in time in the first place. So, did you stop yourself from being born, or did you just create a paradox that collapses the timeline into a weird, never-ending loop? Closed timelike curves, another fun term courtesy of physics suggest that certain events in the timeline could loop around on themselves. So, maybe time travel is possible after all, but only in ways that prevent paradoxes from ever happening. In other words, you could go back in time, but somehow, you’d always end up in a situation where you don’t mess up history. Kind of like the universe is giving you a “free pass” to be awesome. The takeaway? Time travel’s fun in theory, but it might be a headache if we start playing with the past too much. Maybe it’s best to just leave the DeLorean in the garage.

The Arrow of Time: Why Does Time Move Only One Way?
Let’s slow down for a second and ask the next big question: Why does time seem to only move forward? The famous physicist Arthur Eddington called this the Arrow of Time, a one-way direction that seems to be built into the fabric of the universe. You might be thinking, “Okay, cool, time moves forward because that’s just how clocks work.” But here’s the kicker, there’s no actual rule in physics that says time has to move forward. The laws of physics work the same way whether you go forward or backward. So why does time feel like it’s always marching forward? This question ties into entropy, or the second law of thermodynamics. Simply put, entropy is the measure of disorder in the universe. Over time, things tend to move from a state of order to a state of disorder. So, in the big picture, time moves forward because the universe is slowly becoming more disordered. Basically, everything’s gradually falling apart in a predictable way. Nice, right?

Time and the Mind: Does Time Exist Without Us?
Okay, last big question, does time even exist without someone to observe it? Some philosophers argue that time is just a human construct, something we’ve created to make sense of the world around us. After all, if there’s no one there to experience time, does it even matter? Immanuel Kant thought that time and space were part of the way our minds organize experiences, not things that exist independently. According to him, time is part of the mental framework through which we perceive events, it’s a tool we use to make sense of the world. Maybe it’s just a cosmic illusion.

Time is one of those eternal mysteries that philosophers love to ponder because it messes with the fabric of how we experience existence. Whether it’s traveling back to prevent embarrassing moments, or staring at the clock wondering where all those hours went, time is that constant companion we can’t escape and that’s what makes it so fascinating. So, next time you’re stressed about time running out, just remember that time is a bigger mystery than you ever thought. And maybe, just maybe, we’re all just part of the universe’s greatest clockwork show.
