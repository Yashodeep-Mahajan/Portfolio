The Philosophy of Free Will: Are You Really in Control, or Just Along for the Ride?

By Yashodeep Mahajan | March 29, 2020

Ah, free will. That age old concept that makes us believe we’re the masters of our own destiny. We like to think we make our own choices: we choose our career, our relationships, what to have for lunch, and even the next episode of that Netflix show (let’s be real, we always watch one more episode). But deep down, we’ve all had that nagging thought of what if I’m not actually making those choices? What if the decisions I think are mine are actually being influenced by forces beyond my control? In the world of philosophy, free will is a topic so intriguing that it has scholars arguing over it like it’s the ultimate game of chess. Is free will real, or is it just an illusion? And, if we do have it, how free are we really? Buckle up, folks, because we’re diving deep into the philosophy of free will and trust me, it’s not as simple as choosing between pizza or tacos.

The Basics: What Is Free Will
At its core, free will is the idea that we have the ability to make choices independent of outside influences. It’s the sense that, despite our circumstances, desires, or genetic predispositions, we can still choose our actions. In other words, you could decide to take the road less traveled, or you could take the highway to your destination. The question is, are you really deciding it, or is it all just a result of a chain of events that’s already been set in motion? The concept sounds empowering, right? It’s like the universe gave you a blank canvas, and you’re the one holding the paintbrush. But here’s where things get tricky, there’s this giant philosophical question looming over free will, is it compatible with determinism? Determinism is the idea that everything in the universe, including human actions, is determined by preceding events and natural laws. If you’ve ever felt like you’ve been caught in a never-ending loop of making the same bad decisions, you’ve probably wondered if your life is just one big predetermined outcome. But let’s not get ahead of ourselves. There’s more to unpack here.

Determinism: The Cosmic Script You Didn’t Know You Were Following
So, let’s talk about determinism, the idea that the universe is a massive chain of cause and effect. Every event, action, and decision is a result of the events that preceded it. Your childhood, your environment, your genetics and everything that’s happened to you up to this point has shaped the person you are and the choices you make. If you think about it, it sounds a little like life is a script, and you’re just an actor performing your lines, whether you realize it or not. This brings us to the problem of determinism vs. free will. If determinism is true, does that mean free will is just an illusion? If every action we take is already determined by our biology, upbringing, and past experiences, are we really choosing anything at all? Or is the feeling of choice just our mind’s way of making sense of a world that’s already decided for us?

Compatibilism: The “I Can Have My Cake and Eat It Too” Argument
Enter compatibilism, the philosopher’s way of having their cake and eating it too. Compatibilists argue that free will and determinism are not mutually exclusive. They believe that even if our actions are influenced by external forces, we still have the capacity to make choices, as long as we’re not being coerced. In short, we can be determined in certain ways and still be free. It’s like saying, “Sure, I’m influenced by the things around me, but I can still decide whether or not to jump into a pool of ice water for fun.” Compatibilism gives a little more credit to our sense of autonomy while acknowledging that we’re not exactly the lone, independent agents we’d like to think we are. So, when you finally decide to cancel your gym membership for the fifth time (hey, no judgment here), you can still take ownership of that choice even if your past failure to stick with workouts had some influence.

Hard Determinism: Sorry, You’re Not in Control (Really)
Now, on the other end of the spectrum, we’ve got hard determinism. This is the belief that free will doesn’t exist at all, and everything in life is strictly controlled by causes beyond our control. If hard determinism is true, that means you have zero control over your actions. Your thoughts, desires, and decisions are all a consequence of past events, your biology, and the laws of nature. That pizza you ordered? Yep, you didn’t really decide that. It was predetermined by a series of factors beyond your control like your love for cheese, the time of day, and the fact that pizza is just... well, really delicious. Hard determinism can be a bit of a downer, especially when you’re sitting there thinking about your mistakes and wondering if you could have done better. But before you start blaming your entire life on your upbringing and the fact that your parents never made you drink enough water, let’s take a look at libertarian free will, which might just offer a little light at the end of the tunnel.

Libertarian Free Will: You’re the Captain of Your Own Ship
Libertarianism, in the context of free will, suggests that we are genuinely free to make choices that are not determined by external forces. In other words, you can truly decide what to do, independent of your past experiences or biology. It’s like being the captain of your own ship, sailing freely across the vast ocean of choices. This view places a lot of emphasis on personal responsibility. After all, if you’re the one making all the choices, then you’re also the one who has to deal with the consequences. The good news is, if you mess up, you can just blame your poor decision-making skills. The bad news is, if you succeed, you’re also entirely responsible for that.

At the end of the day, the philosophy of free will leaves us with more questions than answers. Are we the captains of our own ships, or are we just passengers on a cruise we didn’t book? Is free will an illusion, or is it just the way we experience the world as we navigate our choices? In the grand scheme of things, we may never know for sure whether we truly have free will, or if we’re just playing a role in a universe of cause and effect. But one thing’s for certain, if you ever find yourself agonizing over the choice between chocolate or vanilla, just remember whether or not you make the “right” decision is part of your philosophical journey. And hey, if you end up with vanilla, at least you can always convince yourself that it was your choice.
